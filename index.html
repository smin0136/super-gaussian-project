Abstract

Despite the promise of virtual reality (VR) for intuitive spatial interaction, volume visualization (VolVis) in VR remains constrained by high rendering costs and motion discomfort. Recent advances have shown that representing volumetric scenes with 3D Gaussian splatting enables high-performance rendering, mitigating the computational latency typically associated with immersive 3D data exploration, thus making this representation well-suited for VR. However, existing Gaussian-based scene editing workflows remain limited by slow offline segmentation and fatigue-inducing manual selection.

To address these challenges, we present Super-Gaussian, a novel VolVis framework that enhances scene editing and interaction in VR through intuitive 3D Gaussian selection and natural language interaction (NLI). Our approach groups Gaussian primitives into higher-level units via feature-aware clustering, enabling efficient selection of complex volumetric regions, such as tumors in medical images or filaments in cosmological data, without tedious point-by-point interaction.

Building on this representation, we introduce a hierarchical select-and-refine workflow that combines random-walk-based region propagation, cluster selection, and point refinement, allowing users to progressively specify regions of interest with reduced effort. We further support on-the-fly text labeling of user-selected regions using NLI, allowing users to semantically query, interpret, and manipulate selected content within a visualization-perception-action loop.

Overview

The main technical ideas in the current submission.

Structure-aware ROI selection

Super-Gaussian clusters Gaussian splats into higher-level interaction units and supports random walk, cluster, and point modes within one coarse-to-fine workflow.

NLI inside VR

Users can issue open-vocabulary commands to label, query, and edit selected regions while continuing direct spatial interaction with the volume in the headset.

Editable Gaussian rendering

The system extends editable Gaussian-based volume visualization to immersive VR with real-time control over color, opacity, lighting, and viewpoint.

Case Studies

Representative scenarios spanning natural objects, medical data, simulation data, and anatomy-focused exploration.

Case 1

Bonsai

The bonsai example shows how the workflow separates semantically distinct regions even when they share similar intensity values and would be hard to isolate with a simple transfer function.

Read more

Starting from two base transfer-function models, the user progressively refines the scene by separating the pot, gravel, branches, leaves, grass, and surrounding structures. Random walk is used for coarse separation of the pot, then manual refinement isolates detailed boundaries more precisely.

The paper emphasizes that brush strokes do not need to be exact seeds. Rough foreground and background hints are sufficient to initialize the random-walk selection, after which manual refinement can target detailed or irregular areas.

Case 2

Aneurysm

The aneurysm dataset highlights a medical scenario where the user decomposes the vascular structure into aneurysm sac, parent vessel, and aneurysm neck, then uses NLI for surgical-style exploration.

Read more

The paper frames the aneurysm neck as especially important because it is the region targeted during clipping. By assigning labels to the selected components, users can issue natural language commands that jointly manipulate all regions associated with aneurysm-related structures.

One example command asks the system to simulate aneurysm clip surgery by eliminating the aneurysm-related area. The response lowers the opacity of the aneurysm components, allowing clearer inspection of the remaining vessel structure.

Case 3

Nyx

The Nyx example demonstrates iterative interpretation of cosmological volume data by separating a dense core, elongated filaments, and isolated fragments that are hard to distinguish in semi-transparent renderings.

Read more

The workflow incrementally classifies visible structures by geometry rather than relying on predefined semantics. Dense central regions are labeled as the core, elongated branches as filaments, and scattered fragments as isolated structures.

The paper uses this case to argue that even spatially disconnected regions can be grouped under one semantic label. Once labeled, they become easy to filter or manipulate through NLI.

Case 4

Chameleon

The chameleon case focuses on perception-guided interpretation. The system uses the current VR view plus the user query to explain selected anatomical structures and support guided-tour style exploration.

Read more

A user may highlight a selected structure and ask whether the green region is the shoulder girdle or the clavicle of a chameleon. The system captures the view, forwards the image and query to the language model, and returns an anatomical interpretation grounded in the visible context.

The paper also describes a guided-tour interaction in which the user asks the system to iteratively explain and recolor segmented parts. This turns user-defined labels into a presentation and explanation interface rather than only an editing mechanism.

User Study

Scope

Two separate studies were conducted. Study 1 evaluated overall framework usability in VR. Study 2 compared the proposed selection technique against existing Gaussian-based baselines.

11 participants 22-31 years old Meta Quest 3 RTX 3090

Hypotheses

  • H1: The method improves accuracy, preference, and fatigue relative to 2D and point-based selection.
  • H2: The hierarchical select-and-refine workflow reduces workload.
  • H3: Selection plus text labeling and NLI improves understanding of unknown structures.
  • H4: VR exploration improves understanding, selection, and editing of volumetric structures.

Framework usability

Participants explored the chameleon, aneurysm, and Nyx datasets after a 15-35 minute training session covering rotation, translation, scaling, brush interaction, text labeling, and NLI.

Participants, setup, and study design

The paper reports 11 unpaid participants from a local university, aged 22 to 31. One participant was left-handed and three wore glasses. All participants reported normal vision and no color perception difficulties.

Prior VolVis experience was mixed: three participants had none, five had experience with DVR tools, and three had prior research experience in volume visualization. VR experience was also mixed, with seven participants reporting no prior experience, one limited experience, and three familiarity with VR interaction.

The study used a Meta Quest 3 connected through wired Link to a desktop VR setup with 2064 × 2208 resolution per eye, 110-degree field of view, and up to 120 Hz refresh rate. The host machine used an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X, 128 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.

Study 1 raw notes: procedure and outcomes

Study 1 evaluated framework usability using the chameleon, aneurysm, and Nyx datasets. A training session was provided to familiarize participants with rotation, translation, scaling, brush interaction, text labeling, and NLI. Training used the bonsai dataset and lasted approximately 15 to 35 minutes, depending on prior VR experience.

After training, participants completed tasks spanning several scenarios: precise region selection and editing of vascular structures, semantic understanding and open-ended segmentation of anatomical models, and exploratory analysis of abstract volumetric data through semantic querying, visual adjustment, and refinement of meaningful regions.

The usability questionnaire covered five categories: selection usability, exploration support, spatial understanding, NLI interaction, and overall experience. The paper reports mean category scores of 6.20, 6.44, 6.60, 6.03, and 6.60 respectively on a 7-point scale.

Participants described the selection interaction as intuitive and accurate, and the hierarchical select-and-refine workflow was rated positively for improving results and supporting error correction. Exploration and spatial understanding received especially strong scores, with participants reporting better perception of 3D structures than with 2D interfaces.

Study 2 raw notes: task design, metrics, and interpretation

Study 2 compared Super-Gaussian against SuperSplat and GSVR on vortex, lobster, and ionization. Participants were first introduced to the target selection task and instructed to select regions highlighted in red. The method order was randomized, and a separate beetle dataset was used for practice before the main trials.

In the VR conditions, both GSVR and Super-Gaussian used controller-based volume manipulation including rotation, translation, and scaling. SuperSplat used mouse-and-keyboard controls, with shortcut keys and modifier-based refinement enabled.

Accuracy was evaluated using ACC, MCC, F1, IoU, and precision. The paper explicitly notes that MCC is useful here because target regions are often small relative to the full point set, which makes class imbalance a concern. Interaction effort was evaluated through interaction count and completion time, and workload was measured with NASA-TLX.

The paper concludes that Super-Gaussian consistently improves overall efficiency, requires fewer interactions and shorter completion times, and reduces workload. It also notes that VR-based methods are especially beneficial on thin or structurally subtle regions such as those in the lobster dataset.

Post-interview summary and implications

Participants naturally adopted a coarse-to-fine strategy, beginning with broad region selection through random walk or cluster selection, then refining boundaries with point-based interaction. This is presented as qualitative support for the intended workflow design.

Selection behavior adapted to dataset characteristics: simpler or well-separated regions were often handled with coarse methods, while denser and more complex structures required cluster and point refinement together. Compared with traditional point-based tools, participants described the system as more efficient and less physically demanding.

The interviews also reinforce the role of VR for spatial understanding and manipulation. NLI was generally viewed as beneficial for interpreting and querying unfamiliar structures, though the paper notes that better latency, readability, undo support, and clearer tool feedback would further improve the experience.

Rendering efficiency notes

The paper reports average rendering performance near 120 FPS across all datasets, comfortably above the 90 FPS threshold commonly cited for VR comfort. Reported datasets range from 234 MB to 4.22 GB in original volume size, while the editable Gaussian representation remains comparatively compact.

Examples include 12.6 MB of splats for the 4 GB Nyx dataset and 25.8 MB for the 4.22 GB chameleon dataset. The authors use these results to argue that Gaussian-splatting-based VolVis can maintain real-time immersive interaction even for large volumes.
