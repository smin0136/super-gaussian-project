Abstract

Despite the promise of virtual reality (VR) for intuitive spatial interaction, volume visualization (VolVis) in VR remains constrained by high rendering costs and motion discomfort. Recent advances have shown that representing volumetric scenes with 3D Gaussian splatting enables high-performance rendering, mitigating the computational latency typically associated with immersive 3D data exploration, thus making this representation well-suited for VR. However, existing Gaussian-based scene editing workflows remain limited by slow offline segmentation and fatigue-inducing manual selection.

To address these challenges, we present Super-Gaussian, a novel VolVis framework that enhances scene editing and interaction in VR through intuitive 3D Gaussian selection and natural language interaction (NLI). Our approach groups Gaussian primitives into higher-level units via feature-aware clustering, enabling efficient selection of complex volumetric regions, such as tumors in medical images or filaments in cosmological data, without tedious point-by-point interaction.

Building on this representation, we introduce a hierarchical select-and-refine workflow that combines random-walk-based region propagation, cluster selection, and point refinement, allowing users to progressively specify regions of interest with reduced effort. We further support on-the-fly text labeling of user-selected regions using NLI, allowing users to semantically query, interpret, and manipulate selected content within a visualization-perception-action loop.

Overview

Super-Gaussian extends editable Gaussian-based volume visualization to immersive VR by introducing structure-aware interaction units, a hierarchical select-and-refine workflow, and NLI-based scene understanding and editing. The project page below follows the paper structure more closely than the earlier summary layout.

The central idea is to move from point-wise Gaussian editing toward region-level interaction. The method groups Gaussian primitives into feature-aware clusters, supports random walk, cluster, and point selection modes, and lets users define, label, query, and manipulate regions of interest directly inside VR.

Extended Overview

Key capabilities

First, the framework supports structure-aware ROI selection without requiring pre-segmented object labels. Second, it integrates NLI into the immersive workflow so that region-specific commands can be issued while the user keeps interacting with the volume. Third, it maintains editable rendering, allowing color, opacity, and lighting modifications at interactive rates.

In the paper, these capabilities are framed as three complementary outcomes: spatially guided interpretation of volumetric structures, iterative perception through selection plus querying, and flexible communication or presentation workflows built on user-defined labels.

Case Studies

The paper presents four case studies to show how Super-Gaussian extends NLI-driven volume visualization toward richer exploratory analysis and presentation workflows. The authors highlight three main themes: spatially guided interpretation through partial Gaussian selection, iterative exploration through the combination of selection and language queries, and communication workflows based on interactive region specification.

5.1 Bonsai

The bonsai dataset is used to demonstrate the usefulness of the select-and-refine workflow when semantically different structures share similar intensity values and cannot be separated cleanly by a simple transfer function. Starting from two base transfer-function models, the user progressively refines the visualization by separating the pot, gravel, branches, leaves, grass, and surrounding structures.

Random walk is first used to separate the pot region from the surrounding structure using only rough foreground and background brush strokes. The paper stresses that these strokes do not need to be precise seeds. After the initial separation, the user performs additional manual refinement to isolate irregular boundaries more accurately. Leaves and grass, which may be similar in value but separated in space, can then be selected and recolored in a step-by-step workflow.

5.2 Aneurysm

The aneurysm example shows how VR-based selection and label-driven editing can support analysis of clinically meaningful vascular structures. The user decomposes the volume into the aneurysm sac, the parent vessel, and the aneurysm neck, then assigns labels to those connected structures while rotating and navigating the volume.

The paper identifies the aneurysm neck as especially important because it is the surgical target during clipping. Once those components are labeled, the user can issue natural language commands that jointly manipulate all aneurysm-related regions, such as reducing their opacity to simulate clipping or removal and reveal the remaining vessel structures more clearly.

5.3 Nyx

In the Nyx dataset, the framework is used to interpret a complex cosmological structure through step-by-step classification and labeling. The user separates a dense central core, elongated filament-like branches, and smaller isolated fragments distributed around the structure.

The paper uses this case to argue that the method is useful even when there are no clear semantic boundaries or predefined labels. Spatially disconnected but conceptually related regions can be grouped under a single label, after which NLI can be used to filter or manipulate them as one category.

5.4 Chameleon

The chameleon example emphasizes perception-guided interpretation. A user may highlight a selected bone region, ask whether the green region is the shoulder girdle or the clavicle, and receive a response grounded in the current VR view captured by the system.

The paper gives examples such as identifying the pectoral girdle or vertebral column from selected colored regions. It also describes a guided-tour interaction in which the user asks the system to explain the segmented parts iteratively across the dataset.

User Studies

Two separate user studies were conducted to evaluate both the performance of the selection method and the usability of the overall framework. Study 1 focused on overall usability in VR, while Study 2 focused on comparative selection performance against existing Gaussian-based methods.

Participants and Experiment Setup

The study recruited 11 unpaid participants aged 22 to 31 from a local university. One participant was left-handed and three wore glasses. All reported normal vision and no difficulties with color perception.

Prior experience was mixed: three participants had no prior VolVis experience, five had experience with DVR tools, and three had prior research experience in volume visualization. For VR, seven had no prior experience, one had limited experience, and three were familiar with VR interaction.

The setup used a Meta Quest 3 headset connected via wired Link to a PC-based VR system with 2064 x 2208 resolution per eye, 110 degree field of view, and up to 120 Hz refresh rate. The desktop machine used an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X CPU, 128 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.

Hypotheses

H1 states that the proposed selection approach should achieve higher accuracy, higher user preference, and lower interaction fatigue than 2D selection and manual point-based selection. H2 states that the hierarchical random-walk, cluster, and point-based workflow should reduce workload by allowing coarse boundary detection followed by fine-grained correction.

H3 states that combining selection with text labeling and NLI-based perception should improve understanding of unknown structures and reduce the number of interactions required. H4 states that VR-based exploration should improve users’ ability to understand, select, and edit volumetric structures.

6.1 Study 1: Framework Usability

Datasets

Study 1 used three datasets: chameleon as a biological dataset, aneurysm as a medical dataset, and Nyx as a simulation dataset.

Task and Procedure

A training session introduced participants to basic VR interaction, including volume rotation, translation, scaling, brush interaction, text labeling, and NLI. The training used the bonsai dataset and lasted approximately 15 to 35 minutes depending on prior VR experience.

After training, participants performed tasks spanning several interaction scenarios: precise region selection and editing of vascular structures, semantic understanding and open-ended segmentation of anatomical models, and exploratory analysis of abstract volumetric data through semantic querying, visual adjustment, and refinement.

Survey Responses

Usability was evaluated with a Likert-scale questionnaire covering selection usability, exploration support, spatial understanding, NLI interaction, and overall experience. The reported mean scores were 6.20, 6.44, 6.60, 6.03, and 6.60, respectively, on a 7-point scale.

The paper reports that participants found the selection interaction intuitive and accurate, rated the hierarchical select-and-refine workflow positively, and reported effective exploration and improved perception of 3D structure relative to 2D interfaces.

Open detailed usability notes

The paper states that participants with prior VolVis or VR experience, identified as P3, P5, P8, P9, and P11, engaged more actively and reported higher satisfaction. It also highlights that exploration and spatial understanding received especially strong responses, which the authors treat as supporting H3 and H4.

Supplementary-level qualitative analysis is not machine-readable in the provided appendix PDF, but the figures and discussion indicate that the framework was experienced as enjoyable, useful for volumetric exploration, and effective for error correction and refinement.

6.2 Study 2: Interactive Selection

Datasets

Study 2 used vortex, lobster, and ionization. The vortex dataset was chosen for transparent volumes with clear density separation, lobster for thin articulated structures, and ionization for sparse regions with empty space.

Task and Procedure

Participants were instructed to select the predefined target region, highlighted in red, after first exploring the volume to locate it. Three methods were compared: SuperSplat as a 2D screen-space baseline, GSVR as a manual point-based VR baseline, and Super-Gaussian as the proposed method. The method order was randomized, and participants practiced each method using the beetle dataset before the main tasks.

In VR conditions, both GSVR and Super-Gaussian used controller-based rotation, translation, and scaling. In SuperSplat, participants used mouse-and-keyboard controls, including modifier-based refinement shortcuts.

Metrics

Accuracy was measured using ACC, MCC, F1, IoU, and precision. The paper explicitly motivates MCC because selection targets occupy only a small fraction of the data, making class imbalance a concern. Interaction effort was measured with interaction count and completion time, and workload was assessed using NASA-TLX.

Selection Performance

Super-Gaussian achieved the strongest overall balance of accuracy, efficiency, and workload reduction. The paper notes that for clearly bounded targets such as vortex and ionization, baselines can sometimes appear competitive in specific metrics, but the proposed method remains consistently strong across the full evaluation, especially when considering interaction effort and workload alongside accuracy.

Open detailed metric and workload notes

The paper interprets interaction count and completion time together because different interfaces produce different action granularities. In VR, a single brush stroke can vary substantially in duration, so count alone does not fully capture physical effort. This is one reason the authors also emphasize workload results and qualitative feedback.

The reported workload plot shows Super-Gaussian with the strongest perceived performance score at 6.60 and substantially lower mental demand, temporal demand, effort, and frustration than the baseline methods. The authors use these findings as support for H1 and H2.

6.3 Post-Interview Summary

Participants naturally adopted a coarse-to-fine strategy, beginning with broad region selection through random walk or cluster modes and then refining with point-based interaction. Selection behavior also adapted to dataset characteristics, with simple or well-separated regions often handled through coarse methods and denser structures requiring cluster and point refinement together.

The post-interview analysis also reinforces the value of VR for depth perception, spatial understanding, and direct manipulation of 3D data. NLI was generally seen as beneficial for understanding unfamiliar structures, though the paper notes that latency, readability, undo support, and clearer visual feedback remain areas for improvement.

Open extended post-interview notes

The paper reports that participants consistently described the multi-method workflow as more efficient and less physically demanding than traditional point-wise editing. It also notes an important nuance: users with strong 2D tool experience sometimes still preferred familiar desktop workflows, even while acknowledging the spatial benefits of VR.

Rendering Efficiency

The paper reports average rendering performance close to 120 FPS across all evaluated datasets, well above the 90 FPS threshold commonly used for VR comfort. This is presented as evidence that Gaussian-splatting-based volume visualization can preserve immersive interaction even for very large volumes.

Examples include 12.6 MB of splats for the 4 GB Nyx dataset and 25.8 MB of splats for the 4.22 GB chameleon dataset. The authors use these results to argue that the representation remains compact while still supporting real-time editable rendering.
